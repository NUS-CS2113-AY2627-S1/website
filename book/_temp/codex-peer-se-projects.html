Comparative course-design report

How peer software engineering courses run projects

A comparison of greenfield and brownfield work, individual and team structures, technology choices, application types, peer evaluation, contribution-aware grading, and AI coding policies.

Executive summary

The dominant design is a staged greenfield team project, preceded by smaller individual or paired work. The strongest examples do not give students a completely blank canvas: they constrain the domain, provide starter infrastructure, release requirements in stages, or require an end-to-end baseline before extensions. This preserves design freedom while keeping novice teams within a tractable problem.

Greenfield work dominates substantial team projects. Brown, MIT, UNSW, Edinburgh, and UT Austin all ask teams to construct a new system, although most provide scaffolding or an evolving specification.

Brownfield learning usually appears in focused exercises. Imperial makes students compare, test, and refactor increasingly large existing systems; Berkeley explicitly includes adding functionality to a legacy codebase. Neither exposes a public, term-long brownfield team-project brief.

Web and client–server systems are the most common application form. Brown, MIT, UNSW, UT Austin, and Berkeley use browser, HTTP, API, SaaS, or cloud contexts. Desktop applications are not the dominant public project form.

Only a few courses publish a concrete method for differentiating team members’ grades. UT Austin and Georgia Tech are the clearest; UNSW gathers rich contribution evidence but keeps its exact adjustment formula private.

Peer evaluation serves three different purposes. It can teach reviewing, alert staff to teamwork problems, or directly affect an individual grade. Treating these as one mechanism obscures important design choices.

AI policy is fragmented, not convergent. Brown integrates AI into the curriculum under an agreement; MIT and UNSW prohibit generated project code; Georgia Tech permits ideation and explanation but prohibits generated submissions and IDE code generation.

The most interesting overall tension is between authenticity and attribution. Larger, realistic teams make coordination more authentic, but they also make individual grading harder. The courses with the clearest accountability combine several signals—peer reports, Git activity, demonstrations, mentor check-ins, reflection, and direct questioning—rather than equating contribution with commit count.

How courses attribute grades within teams

The courses reveal four materially different accountability designs. The choice is not merely whether to “have peer evaluation”; it is what decision the evidence is allowed to drive.

1

Shared grade, strong observation

MIT gives every member the same project grade. It counters free-riding through a prescribed cross-cutting division of work, five graded mentor check-ins, Git-log inspection, an individual reflection, and the possibility of asking for an accounting of work.

This model prioritizes collaboration over competitive point allocation, but relies on staff capacity and does not formally reward unequal contributions.

2

Diagnostic peer evaluation

UNSW explicitly says its teamwork evaluation never directly affects marks. It is a repeated early-warning mechanism for tutors. Individual marks may instead draw on observable evidence: check-ins, demonstrations, GitLab activity, peer reviews, university email, and approved team communication.

This separates honest developmental feedback from grade bargaining, although the private adjustment formula reduces transparency to outsiders.

3

Direct, quantified peer allocation

UT Austin turns contribution judgments into cooperation points. Each student allocates 20 points among teammates for every project phase, cannot award points to themselves, and receives the sum assigned by peers.

A student whose average remains at or below 50% in every phase loses one-third of a course letter grade. This is unusually explicit, but it creates incentives for strategic point trading and requires careful handling of collusion or interpersonal bias.

4

Peer score plus instructor judgment

Georgia Tech gives CATME evaluations a visible 10% course weight. CATME results, reflections, and Git activity can also support a request for a grade adjustment or team change, with the instructor making the final decision.

This is the clearest hybrid: a routine peer-assessment grade plus an evidence-based escalation path for serious imbalance.

Peer review is not always teammate rating

MIT grades the quality of reviews students give on individual problem sets. Edinburgh assesses a group’s review of another group’s code and tests, and individual engagement with professional-issues posts. These mechanisms teach evaluation skill; they do not directly estimate each teammate’s share of the product.

Commit counts are supporting evidence

UT Austin publishes commits, issues, tests, and responsibilities on the application itself. MIT and UNSW inspect repository history. Georgia Tech admits Git activity as evidence. None of the stronger designs treats raw commit count alone as contribution.

Individual assessment can bound team risk

Imperial places 80% on an individual practical exam. Illinois uses individual programming problems, quizzes, and a final exam alongside its 40% team project. These designs verify individual competence without needing to perfectly decompose the team artifact.

How AI coding is used or allowed

The public evidence supports four AI-policy categories, and only four courses expose enough detail to place confidently. A course mentioning AI in teaching does not necessarily allow AI-generated coursework.

Integrated and governed Brown Productive and responsible AI use is a learning goal, backed by an AI-use agreement and dedicated teaching activities. Students encounter AI as a software-engineering subject, not only as an academic-integrity exception. Exact permission boundaries remain non-public.
Learn about AI; do not generate code UNSW Guest teaching and a course chatbot coexist with an explicit ban on ChatGPT-, Copilot-, Gemini-, or similar generated project code. No generated code or text MIT ChatGPT, Copilot, advanced code completion, and generated code/text are prohibited for coursework. The restriction sits inside a detailed collaboration policy, making AI assistance analogous to receiving impermissibly specific help.
Ideation allowed; authored submission required Georgia Tech Students may brainstorm, explore ideas, or clarify concepts, but cannot submit generated content or use tools that insert code into the workspace. The policy asks students to separate the AI conversation from the act of writing, treating AI like preparatory reading or discussion.
AI discussed, permission unclear Washington Course material uses AI-assisted development to motivate precise specifications, but the public pages do not expose an assignment-use rule. AI changes the rationale for a traditional topic without necessarily changing project mechanics.

A useful policy should distinguish at least four activities: asking for explanations, brainstorming designs, generating submitted code, and integrating an AI model or API as a feature of the product. UNSW’s design makes the last distinction especially visible.

Course profiles and interesting mechanics

These profiles emphasize project mechanics rather than general syllabus coverage.

Brown University

CSCI 0320 Introduction to Software Engineering

Project arc
Nine one-week, course-defined sprints followed by a substantial group term project.
Starting point
Early sprints build progressively; the final project is proposed, requirements-gathered, designed, and implemented by students.
Delivery
Alternating recorded and synchronous sprint demos, then a public final demo.
Tech
TypeScript, React, APIs, cloud/Firebase, advanced testing, security and accessibility.

Interesting mechanic: the course front-loads nine tightly bounded rehearsals, then opens the design space. Students reach the greenfield project having already practiced a cadence of implementation and demonstration.

Sources: Fall 2025 course site, current listing.

Massachusetts Institute of Technology

6.102 Software Construction

Project arc
Five individual alpha–review–beta problem sets, then a short team-of-three project.
Starting point
A provided Star Battle domain, sample puzzles, starter files, and a detailed behavioral specification; the design and implementation are new.
Delivery
Team contract, two iterations, five mentor check-ins, final repository, individual reflection.
Tech
TypeScript, Node/Express, browser canvas, Git, Mocha, parsing and networking.

Interesting mechanic: two students independently draft each component in iteration 0; the third owns iteration 1 of that component. The prescribed rotation ensures every student contributes across design, testing, and implementation rather than becoming only “the front-end person” or “the tester.”

Sources: course structure and grading, project brief, collaboration and AI policy.

UNSW Sydney

COMP1531 Software Engineering Fundamentals

Project arc
A nine-week, three-iteration project in teams of five, worth 80% with no final exam.
Starting point
New service built in a course repository; later starter changes arrive as merge requests and can create deliberate merge conflicts.
Delivery
Pre-submission previews, automated and manual marks, reruns, iteration demos, teamwork evaluations, final presentation.
Tech
JavaScript/TypeScript, HTTP API, Jest, linting, CI, persistence and deployment.

Interesting mechanic: staff inject changes into a live student repository between iterations. Students must merge the new starter code into their own evolving solution, turning requirements change and integration conflict into assessed experience.

Source: Term 1, 2025 announcement archive.

University of Edinburgh

INFR08032 Software Engineering and Professional Practice

Project arc
Requirements, design, construction/testing, documentation, and professional-issues work across one coursework project.
Starting point
A Java system built from scratch from an incomplete specification, with interpretation and later change.
Delivery
Formative feedback on early requirements/design, team reflection, code/test review of another group, and summative final submission.
Tech
Java, UML, IDE, version control, issue tracking, testing and code review.

Interesting mechanic: requirements ambiguity and requirements change are deliberate features, while legal, ethical, and social concerns are connected to the same technical scenario rather than taught as a separate appendix.

Sources: 2025/26 catalog entry, open lab design.

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

CS 427 Software Engineering I

Project arc
Five individual machine problems and a five-milestone team project.
Starting point
The project brief is private, so greenfield/brownfield and application type cannot be classified.
Delivery
Milestones align with requirements/design, testing, debugging, and maintenance topics over a 16-week term.
Tech
Java/build systems, patterns, testing, debugging, code smells and static analysis.

Interesting mechanic: the five individual machine problems mirror the lifecycle topics used in the team project. This creates individual practice evidence alongside a large six-to-eight person team artifact.

Source: Spring 2025 syllabus.

University of Texas at Austin

CS 373 Software Engineering

Project arc
Three phases, each worth 12% of the course, growing one full-stack web product.
Starting point
Teams choose a useful linked-data topic but must follow an “IMDB-like” three-model architecture.
Delivery
Static cloud site, then database-backed dynamic site, then search/filter/visualization and presentation.
Tech
React, Bootstrap, Flask, SQLAlchemy, PostgreSQL/MySQL, REST/Postman, GitLab CI, cloud, D3 and documentation.

Interesting mechanic: the public About page must expose each member’s responsibilities, commits, issues, and tests. In the last phase, teams visualize another team’s data and critique that product, creating cross-team API consumption and review.

Sources: Summer 2026 project specification, course schedule.

University of Washington

CSE 331 Software Design and Implementation

Project arc
No substantial team project is visible in the Autumn 2025 public material.
Starting point
Individual topic-driven homework, labs, worksheets and exams.
Delivery
A ten-topic progression through specification, correctness, abstraction, mutation, types, generics and recursion.
Tech
Primarily Java, with comparative language-design examples.

Interesting mechanic: this is a useful negative case. A beginner software-engineering course can prioritize deep construction reasoning and individual verification without using a large team project as its organizing device.

Sources: Autumn 2025 topics, calendar.

Imperial College London

COMP50002 Software Engineering Design

Project arc
Weekly paired exercises on progressively larger systems, not one term-long product.
Starting point
Real existing software in which students implement alternatives, test, refactor, and compare trade-offs.
Delivery
Automated feedback plus staff feedback every week; final individual computer-based practical exam.
Tech
TDD, mocks, metrics, patterns, GUI, integration, distribution, web and REST.

Interesting mechanic: practical skill is assessed in its natural environment. Pair work supports learning, but an 80% computer-based individual exam makes personal competence unmistakable.

Source: current module page.

University of California, Berkeley

CS 169A Introduction to Software Engineering

Project arc
Offering-level assignments are private; catalog outcomes combine SaaS development and legacy-code evolution.
Starting point
Mixed: customer-driven service construction plus adding functionality to an existing legacy codebase.
Delivery
Agile/XP, user stories, estimation, BDD/TDD, deployment, refactoring and operations.
Tech
Web/SaaS and public cloud; exact current framework is not public.

Interesting mechanic: legacy-code change is an explicit learning outcome, an uncommon counterweight to the greenfield bias of large student projects.

Source: current official course page.

Georgia Institute of Technology

CS 2340 Objects and Design

Project arc
A substantial Agile team system; setup and implementation together carry 40%.
Starting point
Project brief, codebase status, language, and application domain are private.
Delivery
Sprints, mentor check-ins, CATME, conflict escalation, testing, refactoring and quality feedback.
Tech
Object-oriented architecture, UML, GRASP/SOLID, patterns, version control and SonarQube.

Interesting mechanic: teams can request a membership change before the end of Sprint 2, but must provide evidence such as CATME results, reflections, or Git activity. The escalation window encourages early intervention rather than end-of-term complaints.

Source: Spring 2026 syllabus.

Implications for designing a beginner software engineering project

The peers suggest a practical design space rather than a single best model. The following ideas appear especially portable.

  1. Use constrained greenfield rather than a blank canvas. Fix the domain shape, interfaces, minimum end-to-end behavior, or technology boundaries while leaving architecture and implementation choices to students. MIT and UT Austin show two strong versions.
  2. Add one real brownfield experience. A focused lab that requires reading, testing, refactoring, or extending unfamiliar code can cover a professional reality that a student-owned project rarely reproduces. Imperial and Berkeley provide the clearest rationale.
  3. Separate peer feedback from peer punishment. Repeated low-stakes teamwork surveys can surface issues early, as at UNSW. If peer input affects grades, publish the decision rule and retain instructor review, as UT Austin and Georgia Tech do in different ways.
  4. Triangulate contribution evidence. Combine planned task ownership, Git history, issue activity, demos, check-ins, reflection, and oral questioning. Raw commits are too easy to game and undervalue design, review, debugging, mentoring, and coordination.
  5. Require breadth of contribution. MIT’s rotation across component design, tests, and implementation prevents premature specialization and gives each student evidence of multiple engineering skills.
  6. Inject change deliberately. UNSW’s staff-authored merge requests and Edinburgh’s changing requirements let students experience integration and change without the uncontrolled risk of a genuine external client.
  7. Keep an individual verification channel. Individual programming work, practical assessment, reflection, or a short oral defense limits uncertainty about personal attainment. Imperial, Illinois, and MIT each demonstrate a different mechanism.
  8. Write AI policy by activity, not by tool name alone. State separately whether students may seek explanations, brainstorm, generate submitted code, refactor with AI, create tests with AI, and call an AI service from their product. Tool lists age quickly; activity boundaries express the learning intent.

Method, evidence quality, and limitations

This report starts from the ten-course review in _temp/peer-courses.md and follows its official course links where public mechanics needed clarification. It favors 2025 and 2026 offerings and does not infer hidden assignment rules from course titles or older folklore.

  • Strongest project evidence: MIT and UT Austin publish detailed specifications; UNSW publishes a rich operational announcement trail; Georgia Tech publishes contribution and AI policies.
  • Moderate evidence: Brown publishes its sprint cadence and course outcomes but not the final project rubric; Edinburgh and Illinois publish structure but not current deliverable weights or full briefs.
  • Limited offering-level evidence: Berkeley exposes rich outcomes but hides assignments; Washington exposes rich teaching material but no substantial team project; Imperial exposes exercise and assessment design but not examples or a language.
  • “Not public” is a finding, not a negative policy claim. A course may have detailed team, grading, or AI rules inside Canvas, Coursera, bCourses, Gradescope, or another authenticated system.
  • Peer evaluation and peer review are kept distinct. Rating teammate contribution, reviewing another student’s code, and critiquing another team’s product have different learning and grading functions.

Bottom line: the most fully specified peer projects combine bounded greenfield construction, staged delivery, observable individual activity, repeated feedback, and a separately stated AI boundary. The least common—but potentially most valuable—addition is meaningful brownfield work on an unfamiliar codebase.
